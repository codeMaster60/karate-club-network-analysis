The Karate Club Split

Between 1970 and 1972, anthropologist Wayne Zachary recorded who was friends with whom among 34 members of a university karate club. Then a dispute between the instructor and the club's administrator broke the club in two. Can network metrics alone find the people who mattered, and predict which side each member chose?

Part 2 · Comparing the metrics

Same two leaders, different winners

Every metric puts members 0 and 33 at the top. They disagree on who comes first, because they measure different kinds of importance.

Top five members by each metric

How similar are the rankings?

Spearman rank correlation. 1.00 means the two metrics rank all 34 members in the same order.

The Officer wins on popularity and influence (degree, eigenvector, PageRank). Mr. Hi wins on reach and brokerage (closeness, betweenness). Degree and PageRank are almost the same ranking (0.98). Eigenvector is the most different: it rewards sitting inside the well-connected core, while betweenness rewards bridging between groups.

Part 3 · In-degree and out-degree

When ties have a direction

Friendship in the karate club is mutual, so the network has no direction and in/out-degree do not apply. This small who-follows-whom network shows the idea.

Ravi is the most popular (4 people follow him) but follows only one person. Eshan is active (follows 2) but nobody follows him back, so his PageRank is the lowest. In-degree measures popularity; out-degree measures activity.

Part 5 · Robustness

What if the central members leave?

Remove members in order of a metric and watch the club fall apart. Faded members have been removed; the largest group that can still reach each other is shown in colour.

Largest connected group as members are removed

Removing the top 4 members by degree, betweenness, eigenvector or PageRank leaves only 29% of the club connected. Removing 4 random members leaves about 86%. Removing Mr. Hi first does far more damage than removing the Officer first (79% vs 97% remaining), which confirms he is the club's main bridge.

Conclusions

What the metrics told us

0 & 33

Every metric finds the two leaders

From friendship structure alone, all five metrics place Mr. Hi and the Officer at the top: the two people at the centre of the real conflict.

44%

Mr. Hi holds the club together

He lies on 44% of all shortest paths and is the only member whose removal cuts anyone off: without him, 6 members lose all contact with the club. The Officer has more friends, but Mr. Hi is the better connector.

97%

Structure predicted the split

Siding with the nearer leader predicts 33 of 34 members. Girvan–Newman, which cuts high-betweenness ties, recovers the split with 94% accuracy.

4

A few members carry the network

Losing 4 central members disconnects most of the club; losing 4 random members barely matters.

Limitation. Centrality describes a member's position, not their reasons. The one member every model misplaces, member 8, joined Mr. Hi for a personal reason: Zachary reports he was close to his black-belt exam under Mr. Hi.

Data and method. Zachary's Karate Club: 34 members, 78 friendships, faction labels. Analysis in Python with NetworkX; full code and explanations are in the project notebook (notebooks/karate_sna.ipynb). Members are numbered 0–33 as in NetworkX; add 1 for the numbering in Zachary's paper.
