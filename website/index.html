The Karate
Club Split

From 1970 to 1972, anthropologist Wayne Zachary recorded who was friends with whom among the 34 members of a university karate club. Then a dispute between the instructor and the club's administrator broke the club in two. Using four centrality metrics, we ask: who held this network together, and could its structure have predicted the split?

The network

Each member is a node. Each friendship is an edge.

Zachary counted a friendship when two members met consistently outside the club. Friendship goes both ways, so the network is undirected. Every member's later choice of side is known, which lets us check our metrics against what really happened.

a Core centrality metric a

Degree centrality

Counts the number of direct connections (edges) a node has. In directed networks it splits into indegree (incoming ties, popularity) and outdegree (outgoing ties, activity).From the lab brief

Formula

CD(v) = deg(v) / (n − 1)
  • deg(v) number of friends of member v
  • n − 1 = 33, the most friends anyone could have; dividing scales the score to 0–1

Read it as

How many people can this member talk to directly?

    Indegree and outdegree

    The karate network has no direction, so every tie counts once for both members. On social media, ties often go one way: you can follow someone who does not follow you back. This small follow network shows the two halves of degree.

    b Core centrality metric b

    Closeness centrality

    Measures the average length of the shortest paths from a node to all other nodes in the network. High closeness means a node can quickly interact with or spread information to the entire network.From the lab brief

    Formula

    CC(v) = (n − 1) / Σu d(v, u)
    • d(v, u) fewest steps (friendships) from v to u
    • Σ d(v, u) total steps to reach all 33 other members
    • The inverse of the average distance, so closer = higher

    Read it as

    How quickly can news from this member reach everyone?

    c Core centrality metric c

    Betweenness centrality

    Calculates how often a node falls on the shortest path between other pairs of nodes. Nodes with high scores act as vital bridges or gatekeepers controlling information flow between different clusters.From the lab brief

    Formula

    CB(v) = Σs ≠ v ≠ t σst(v) / σst
    • σst number of shortest paths from s to t
    • σst(v) how many of those pass through v
    • Summed over every pair, then divided by the 528 pairs that exclude v, giving 0–1

    Read it as

    If two members talk along the shortest route, how often must the message pass through this member?

    Or click two members

    Who is on everyone's path?

    Repeat the path count above for all 561 pairs of members and add up each member's share. That total is betweenness. Two members carry most of the traffic.

      d Core centrality metric d

      Eigenvector centrality

      Assigns relative scores to all nodes based on the principle that connections to high-scoring (well-connected) nodes contribute more to the score of the node in question. PageRank is a famous variant of this concept.From the lab brief

      Formula

      xv = (1/λ) Σu ∈ N(v) xu  ⇔  A x = λ x
      • xv score of member v
      • N(v) v's friends; A the adjacency matrix
      • λ the largest eigenvalue; x is its eigenvector

      Read it as

      Is this member friends with important people?

      Watch the scores settle

        PageRank, the famous variant

        Google's algorithm imagines a reader who follows a random friendship with probability d, or jumps to any random member with probability 1 − d. A member's score is the share of time the reader spends there. Each member splits its score evenly among its friends.

        PR(v) = (1 − d)/n + d · Σu ∈ N(v) PR(u) / deg(u)

        At d = 0 everyone scores the same (1/34). The standard value is 0.85. Drag it and watch the hubs grow.

Degree and PageRank are almost the same ranking; eigenvector differs most, because it rewards sitting inside the core rather than bridging between groups.

          Findings

          What the metrics told us

          0 · 33

          Every metric finds the leaders

          From friendships alone, all five metrics put Mr. Hi and the Officer in their top two: the two people at the centre of the real dispute.

          44%

          Mr. Hi holds the club together

          He lies on 44% of all shortest paths. Remove him and 6 members lose all contact with the club. The Officer has more friends; Mr. Hi is the better bridge.

          33/34

          Structure predicted the split

          "Join the nearer leader" predicts 33 of 34 members. Girvan–Newman, which cuts high-betweenness ties, gets 32.

          29%

          A few members carry everyone

          Remove the top 4 by betweenness and only 29% of the club stays connected. Remove 4 at random and about 86% does.

          Data and method

          Zachary's Karate Club: 34 members, 78 friendships and each member's faction. All metrics computed in Python with NetworkX (code and notebook in the project repository) and checked against the Network Repository's published statistics. The interactive parts of this page recompute closeness, shortest paths, eigenvector centrality and PageRank live in your browser. Members are numbered 0–33; add 1 for the numbering in Zachary's paper.
